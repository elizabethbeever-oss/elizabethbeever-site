About Elizabeth

Healthcare technology is increasingly capable of doing things that would have seemed extraordinary only a few years ago. But whether those capabilities translate into better healthcare depends on the decisions made about how technology is designed, evaluated and used.

My perspective has been shaped by more than a decade in clinical dentistry, working across different healthcare settings in the UK and New Zealand. Clinical practice teaches you that good decisions depend on more than having the right information. They require judgement, an understanding of the person in front of you and an appreciation of the consequences when things go wrong.

That experience now informs my work in digital health and responsible AI. I’m particularly interested in the questions that sit between technological capability and clinical reality: what evidence we need before trusting an AI system, how safety and accountability should be built into its development, and how healthcare professionals can be equipped to use these technologies critically and confidently.

Through practical product development work, continued study and published writing, I’m building on that clinical foundation with a focus on AI governance, ethics, digital clinical safety and healthcare innovation.

I want to contribute to a future in which healthcare AI is developed thoughtfully, evaluated rigorously and introduced in ways that genuinely benefit the people expected to use it.

Areas of interest

01

Responsible AI, governance and evaluation

What evidence should we expect before introducing AI into healthcare, and how should we evaluate it once it is in use? I explore questions of accountability, transparency, monitoring and the responsibilities of those developing and deploying these systems.

02

Healthcare product development and clinical safety

Useful healthcare technology starts with understanding the people and problems involved. My interests include user research, product requirements, human factors and how clinical safety considerations inform decisions throughout development.

03

Ethics, AI literacy and professional judgement

Healthcare professionals need to understand both the capabilities and limitations of AI. I write about meaningful human oversight, equitable access and the judgement needed to decide when these tools are appropriate.

Build project 01 · November 2026 Live project

Building and evaluating an AI-assisted professional website

I built this website as a place to bring together my writing, professional interests and practical projects in digital health and responsible AI. I used AI-assisted development tools to help turn the initial concept into a working site, then reviewed and refined the result through hands-on testing.

The work included defining the site's audience and structure, editing its content, managing changes through GitHub, setting up Google Search Console and improving technical SEO. I also used Lighthouse audits to assess mobile performance and accessibility, then made changes to text contrast and accessible link labels.

The first Lighthouse tests returned 100/100 for performance on both mobile and desktop. Accessibility scored 94/100 for the homepage and 92/100 for the first article. Those scores are useful signals, but they do not establish WCAG compliance. Further manual accessibility checks and follow-up testing remain part of the work.

The main lesson has been the importance of checking AI-assisted output against real requirements, making deliberate changes and testing whether those changes have improved the result.
